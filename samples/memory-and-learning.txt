The hippocampus is a small, curved structure deep in the temporal lobe that plays a central role in forming new memories. Damage to the hippocampus, as in the famous case of patient H.M., leaves old memories largely intact but prevents the formation of new long-term memories, a condition called anterograde amnesia. During sleep, the hippocampus replays patterns of activity from the day, and this replay is thought to transfer memories to the neocortex for long-term storage, a process called systems consolidation. Slow-wave sleep is especially important for consolidating declarative memories, which are memories for facts and events.

Retrieval practice, meaning actively recalling information rather than rereading it, strengthens memories more than passive review. In a meta-analysis of 122 experiments, practice testing improved later performance with an effect size of about 0.4 compared with restudying. The spacing effect shows that study sessions spread out over time produce better retention than the same amount of study crammed into one session.

Overconfidence is common in learners: people tend to judge that they know material better than they actually do, especially after rereading it, because fluency of reading is mistaken for knowledge. Metacognitive judgments become more accurate after a test, because the test gives direct evidence of what can and cannot be recalled.

Working memory, which holds information briefly for manipulation, is distinct from long-term memory and depends more on the prefrontal cortex than on the hippocampus. Long-term potentiation, a lasting increase in synaptic strength following repeated stimulation, is considered the main cellular mechanism of learning in the hippocampus.
